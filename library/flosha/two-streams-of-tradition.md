*A common basis on the traditional Idea*
Among the so called traditionalists we have thinkers such as Ivan Agueli, René Guenon, Julius Evola, Ananda Coomaraswamy, Frithjof Schuon. These names are usually and without doubt assigned the label of "*traditionalists*", sometimes Guénon, Coomaraswamy and Schuon are named "*perennialists*" instead in order to differentiate their thought from the fascist ideas of Evola and such modern, superficial thinkers as Aleksandr Dougan.  
It can be controversial to decide who may also belong to this "school" or may not. If it means valueing tradition and the believe into a perennial wisdom and a true core to all religion, then we can count the old mystics and masters from various traditions who attest to what has sometimes be called the religion of love, such as Rumi and Ibn Arabi as well as Meister Eckhart to the same school of thought. And if so we must count Gandhi and Vinoba and Lanza del Vasto, the proponents of Sarvodaya to this "school of thought" too. Coomaraswamy described it as:

> “the universal metaphysical tradition that has been the essential foundation of every past culture”

If it suffices to share the thought that there is such an essential foundation of every past culture and an universal metaphysics that they want to preserve and initiate their action from, then there is no doubt that all those people belong to this "school". But in fact there is no such "school", there is no fixed system that everyone would adhere to. There are simply some fundamental ideas common to all traditional culture that they share, so it is more correctly not to speak of a system or a school, but to just say that they share the traditionalist idea and the idea of perennial wisdom found all over the world. This can be said without doubt.   

*Current Meaninglessness of Conservativism and Progressivism.*
In this context it may also be important to note, that explaining Gandhi and Vinoba to be "traditional", which they clearly are, does not make them in any way "conservative" as opposed to "progressive" or vice versa. These two terms lost all meaning in course of time. They are both. They are conservative, because they want to conserve everything that is valuable and of eternal truth in tradition; but if all that has been lost in the current society, then there is nothing to conserve, and every attempt to re-introduce these values into society might be seen as "progressive" and "revolutionary" instead. If on the other hand those people who now want to maintain the status quo, want to "preserve" the mechanized destruction of industrial civilisation, then they are to be seen as "conservative" from this angle and cannot be called "progressive" in any way, even if at some point in the past they might have been "progressive" and might have destroyed what others wanted to preserve.  

It is therefore also no wonder that some people have described Gandhi as being "right", while others would describe him, an anarchist, as being "extreme left". These two terms too have lost all meaning in our present-day circumstances. It is much simpler than this. Some people believe in truth more than anything and others don't. If there is some truth in what is uttered from the right political camp and if there is some truth in what is uttered from the left political camp, then it is irrelevant from which direction the truth is coming; it only matters that it is true. The only adherence is to truth and not to a party. And thus, it is wrong to describe Gandhi, Vinoba and others, who (Gandhi at least at the end of his life, Vinoba all his life) stood beyond any party and clearly were opposed to become part of any government, as being left or right; they are neither. Nor are they interested in such petty descriptions of a poliitcal position. 


*Disagreements in the political Sphere*
While all of the thinkers mentioned above might agree about the fundamental ideas of tradition and the essence of perennial wisdom, there are some very radical differences between them when it comes to the application of their ideas onto the social sphere and when it comes to their call to action. 

* Ivan Agueli was an anarchist.
* René Guenon tried to keep completely aloof from political affairs.
* Julius Evola called himself a "super-fascist".
* Ananda Coomaraswamy argued for Anarchism again.
* Frithjof Schuon kept out of political affairs, but seemed to lean into the autocratic direction. 
* Gandhi and Vinoba were non-violent anarchists. 
* Lanza del Vasto never attested anarchy as the ideal, as far as I know, but practically founded anarchist communities with a patriarchy (in a biblical sense) only as the outer form, which was not in any conflict with the anarchist idea. 

On first sight it seems like an extreme divide among these thinkers. But in order to understand if that is so and in which sense that is so, we have to first understand what they particularly envisioned and meant with these words.  

*A common Ground in their Critique of Modern "Economy"*:  
They all share a fundamental critique of the current economical system and industrialism and the destruction it causes, both from an environmental perspective in regard to the destruction of nature, as well as from a human perspective in regard to the destruction of culture, such as the destruction of art and craft. It is no wonder that Coomaraswamy and Gandhi, although looking at it from different perspectives, were both influenced by John Ruskin. 

*A common Ground in their Critique of Democracy, but for different reaons and apparently very different Conclusions in regard to Hiararchy*:  
Secondly, they also all share a fundamental critique of the current political system and of democracy. But here with very different conclusions and for different reasons. We could say, some of them attack democracy because it is not hierarchical enough, opening it up to very bad influences from "below", from those who, according to them, shouldn't possess any power as they aren't worthy and capable, while the others attack democracy because they see it as a mere nominal democracy which doesn't really change anything about the hierarchy and does actually *not* give any power into the hands of the people as it should. One part of them arrive at the ideal of an autocratic order on spiritual grounds, while the other part arrives at the ideal of an anarchist order on spiritual grounds.  
It suffices to say, that beyond these two opposed stances, both would probably agree, when we say, that the minorities currently in power cannot be considered to be "capable" in any spiritual sense either, because they aren't serving any kind of higher interests or the interests of all, but only the interests of a financial elite; politics today is completely absorbed and controled by the Mammon.  

*Disagreements about Violence and the Role of Jesus*
Third, they have a fundamental disagreement about heroism, the question of war and violence and it is very striking and surely not without a reason, but the reason may exactly be found in this, that the traditionalists have the most disagreement about the teachings of Jesus. Because some of them consider the teaching of Jesus as a teaching of the weak, as it apparently glorifies the weak, glorifies the poor, condemns wealth, talks about love all the time, tells them to endure suffering instead of fighting against it and so on. But they don't see on which basis it does so and why. They do not see in his teaching the power, the force, that other traditions seem to express. While others consider it as perhaps the strongest expression of power and the sacrifice of Jesus perhaps as the most heroic act, because they have found in it the ancient teaching of non-violence and a way to fight against this world exactly not in the passive way as which the critiques of non-violence like to present it. Satyagraha may be the most important discovery and "invention" of the modern world and the most necessary one, as it contains the seed of overcoming it.

Fourth, a apparently opposed view about "nature". To Evola, the natural is negative, low, far away from the aristocratic soul, that belongs to the heavenly realm of the ideal. While in Coomaraswamy we find nature described like this:

A post-Western, post-industrial future therefore had to be based on the essential beauty and form that comes to us from nature.  
> “To reform what has been deformed means that we must take account of an original ‘form’”, (15) he wrote. This original form, such as an organic, anarchic, just, non-industrial, natural community, was a kind of possibility-in-waiting, which always had the potential of becoming real.

But ultimately, it may again be a misunderstanding that dissolves in works like that of Lanza del Vasto. Lanza too uses phrases such as going against ones nature, having to overcome nature etc., while at the same time, in his communities he very much promoted a "back to nature" in regard to the outer forms of life. In the Buddhist Doctrine we find the idea of going against the grain. Bhikkhu Anigha phrases it as... [???] "one has to go against ones birth [from-memory;add-correct-quote]. Evola climbed on mountains, used mountain climbing as a spiritual journey and surely wasn't unable of seeing nature as a divine manifestation (as unable as he may have been to see a manifestation of the same divine in many other human beings that he classified on racial terms...).  
The critique of the "natural" is to be seen from a specific perspective, which is not necessarily in conflict with living much closer and "in harmony" *with* nature. This outer form of simple living doesn't imply to "open oneself up" to lower forces, to go backwards in terms of spiritual development or anything like that. On the contrary. It is in the sky scrapers, in the modern city where human nature sinks the lowest, is suppressed and suffocates under the technological and economical pressure. While among the simple huts of rural community and under preservation of the dignity of labour in form of art & crafts, human nature can find full expression and rise to true spiritual verticality. The Sarvodaya way is a deliberately "lower" external standard of living, but a much higher internal standard of living.

### External Expression vs. Internal Foundation of Power

In regard to the political problem, there is an important statement by Vinoba in his Swaraj Shastra (7): 

> [...] it is perfectly obvious that *sarvayatan* (“rule of all”) does not exist anywhere today. [...] There is a polity that calls itself ‘democracy’ (“rule of the people”) and poses as ‘*sarvayatan*’ (“rule of all”). Its drama is being staged in Europe and America. But no system based on violence is really “rule of all” [...].
> As against this, if all the people, consciously and willingly, entrust power to one or more of themselves, whom they know to be wise, competent, free from attachment and hatred and devoted to the welfare of all, then that polity, although *ekayatan* (rule by one) or *anekayatan* (rule by more than one) in appearance, should be regarded as *sarvayatan* (rule by all), as it is based on nonviolence. 

The important part here lies in the term "appearance". This is something, that again, the traditionalist thinkers should all be able to understand: It is never about the particular external form that something takes or in which it finds expression, as that says very little about the spiritual foundation it rests upon; an order based on spiritual principles can take many outer forms. If a single individuum is actually "ruling" in a way by which it serves the wellbeing of all, then that individuum is actually the servant of all and is justified in that and sacred to that degree. If no individuum is "ruling" in any way but everyone rules himself in outer form too, then that as well can be a sound spiritual order.  

But in this context again, Vinoba said:
> Even in an autocratic rule of a king, all power is not wielded by him alone. He has some assistants; and the masses too cooperate with him to some extent. That is the reason for sustenance of his rule. On the other hand, even in a *sarvayatan* polity, it is not that everyone directly participates in actual administration. That task is entrusted to a few people, taking for granted the cooperation by all. And some one individual has normally to be regarded as the final authority.

He concludes that, among other factors, every possible kind of political order will have in common that the *"actual administration is in the hands of capable persons"* and that the *"final authority is with a single individual"*. The outer form of the organisation doesn't matter much for this; it will have to be the case anyway, in any kind of order, be it an autocratic order or an anarchist order. What is different is how these capable persons and this single individual are given (or take) that responsibility, what they do, under which rules and on which basis they operate, how capable they actually are and so forth.  
Here lies all the difference and all the justification or condemnation from the spiritual perspective: How the individuals that have responsibility *relate*. How they relate to power, how they relate to themselves and all others. And how these others, how *all* relate to them. It makes a difference if they take responsibility motivated by their own egoic ambitions or if they are entrusted with it like a burden to carry. It makes a difference by which means one can enter such a position. It makes a difference if someone can be elected to it by simple majority votes (which is violent and worse often ends up being minority votes), if he can be appointed by someone, perhaps against the interests of others, or if he has to be choosen by all through consent. 

> “for a form, by definition, cannot be unique and exclusive, that is to say it cannot be the only possible expression of what it expresses”. (Schuon, The Transcendent Unity of Religions)

This statement written by Schuon in regard to Religion is just as true in regard to any other form of any other underlying reality. 

> “The work to be done is primarily one of purgation, to drive out the money changers, all who desire power and office, and all representatives of special interests [...]” (Coomaraswamy)

The *desire* for power and money de-justify it automatically.  
When you have any desire to rule you are already de-justified to rule over others. When you have any desire for money you are already de-justified to possess wealth. 
Then, to use religious language, that rule and that wealth are an affront to God. 

When one desires power, is hungry for power, thirsty for power or if one desires money, is hungry for money, thirsty for money, he is like a father or like a mother who desires to have children for their own, as possessions of themselves, extensions of themselves, and not let them be for themselves and exist on their own and of their own accord.  
In desiring they automatically abuse. There is no desire without abuse and no abuse without desire. Any abuse of others is always grounded in egoic ignorance in regard to the self-being of the other; it is a blind denial of the other as self. The hunger for power and wealth are always a blind denial of the purpose of both. Of power, to be used wisely and to gradually be distributed completely among ones children, which in case of state power, means among everyone, so that everyone is capable to take responsibility for oneself. Of wealth, to be used wisely and to gradually be distributed completely among everyone, so that everyone is capable to have enough. What kind of father is this, who accumulates wealth enough to feed the world and then let the majority of his children starve and turn the rest into pigs? That is exactly the practice of some of the feudal lords of today.

### The Conflict dissolves in *Swaraj*

It is in the ideas of Gandhi and Vinoba, that any kind of conflict between *Autocracy* and *Anarchy* dissolves. Because true *Anarchy* is in fact the *Autocracy* of everyone; in other words: *The rule of none over anyone can only be achieved through self-rule*, the rule of everyone over oneself. And as well: True *Autocracy* over oneself is in fact *Anarchy* in regard to others: Self-rule is only complete if it doesn't have to bother about ruling others. 

> *‘Na twaham kamaye rajyam. Yatemahi swarajye.’* 
> *‘I do not desire to rule. Let us strive for self-rule.’*

It is only here that Marx' vision can find realisation, that has never really be strived for in any communist state, that the state will ultimately wither away and give rise to Anarchy. It is through the realisation of Swaraj. 

And it is also in this sense that any kind of "patriarchy", as well as "matriarchy", both traditional ideas, find their true and non-violent meaning: This is not a father worth the name, this is not a *patriarch*, who doesn't have the intent and doesn't consider it his aim to make all of his children completely independent from him, responsible for themselves (it is in this non-violent way that Lanza del Vasto used the term *Patriarch* and the kind of patriarch he himself embodied, which cannot the slightest be confused with the negative connotations that this word may evoke currently); and this is not a mother worth the name, this is not a *matriarch*, who doesn't have the intent and doesn't consider it her aim to make all of her children completely independent from her, responsible for themselves. 

And it is also this (and only this) what justifies a state: If it attempts and constantly works on making itself unnecessary. Because *that government is the best which governs the least* (Thoreau), but that does not mean that the state and any kind of government has to be violently destroyed right now in order to serve the interest of profit-driven abusive corporations (those nominal "liberals" that serve the interest of none but their own); it means that the state and any kind of government has to start right now to educate the people in self-rule to the best of their ability and thereby reduce its own influence and control, giving the people more and more responsibility for universal cooperation in the *interest of all* (the value of competition is a farce), like a father and a mother; and it is a good state and a good government only to the degree that they do so. And where they don't do so people have to educate themselves and make themselves independent by their own effort, just like a child that has to emancipate itself from the control of its abusive parents, who has to *take* responsibility itself because it wasn't given the responsibility by them that it was meant to be gradually taught. 

The state is justified to the degree that it allows us to take more responsibility ourselves as we show us capable of doing so, always making clear that its own dissolution is the aim. And it is unjustified to the degree that it does not grant us the responsibility we deserve; *then* it is justified to take it, because it isn't theirs that they withhold, it is always ours; it is nothing to take away from them, it is just something to develop in oneself. It is thus always justified to take the responsibility over *ourselves* that they may not grant to us; but it is never justified to take the unjustified power of the state over others away from the state, to then oneself exercise unjustified power over others.

## Against World Trade / Economical Conflicts and Common Grounds

Coomaraswamy described himself as in a battle “against industrialism and world trade”. The consequence of the destruction of the arts and craft and their forceful separation through industrialism, he described as a "spiritual caste system". He writes: “Those who have lost most by this are the artists, professionally speaking, on the one hand, and laymen generally on the other. The artist (meaning such as would still be so called) loses by his isolation and corresponding pride, and by the emasculation of his art, no longer conceived as intellectual, but only as emotional in motivation and significance; the workman (to whom the name of artist is now denied) loses in that he is not called, but forced to labor unintelligently, goods being valued above men”.

Frithjof Schuon wrote:
> “How self-defeating are the aims of humanitarianism is to be observed in the fact that it accepts what is most inhuman, mechanization, which suppresses artisanship, hence one of the conditions of human happiness; there was much merit in Gandhi’s campaign against the machine, and consequently on behalf of human dignity”. (Gnosis: Divine Wisdom)

I think the major mistake in thinkers like Julius Evola is their elitism and a misunderstanding in regard to the role of ancient empires and emperors to tradition. In very simplified terms: While being for the preservation or the revival of tradition, he seems to see the Roman Empire and stuff like that as something that holds up the torch of tradition and of the perennial truth, being a worldy expression of an higher order, while actually the truth and the tradition has never been preserved by the empires of this world; it has always just persisted inspite of them. 
No empire has done tradition or spirituality any good at any time, neither did they create it nor did they preserve it. All true and perennial tradition has been founded and was strengthened and nourished by those who have rejected any external authority. The roman empire was a mere superficial structure within which tradition was practiced and survived, just as it was as well practiced outside of that empire. But spiritually there was little sacred about the particular form of the empire, nor was there anything sacred about its wars against other people. 

At most the more profound and more widespread traditional outlook among the people helped to prevent the empire from becoming too great of a force against the eternal values of tradition and helped to set up structures and mechanisms against this. But it never was dependent from any such political structure. 

In the same way, the christian tradition, if it survived at all in what it essentially meant to be, survived inspite of the Christianisation by Power through the State and not because of it; there was never any greater harm done to the christian tradition and the true expression of christian faith than by this. And if there has been valuable political structures e.g. in medieval times, if there has been good institutions and political rules that have been based on christian grounds, by influence of the church etc., then that is not to be seen as political power to be anything essential in a traditionalist society, but simply as the attempts of religious figures to limit and control the political sphere, to restrict corruption that would otherwise devour everything if being unrestricted, as it is happening now. The perennial wisdom was uphold inspite of these powers, not because of time, often against these powers. And even if it succeeded momentarily to limit the corruption, eventually by seeking power and money themselves, all institutionalised religion was corrupted too, to the extent that now institutionalised religion is over and cannot be rectified anymore. The end result has always been that all of the worldly empires collapsed, as they were based on violence and were violent to the core; none of these empires was sacred and justified; not at the time of their collapse at least. And sooner or later the same will happen inevitably to the violent empires of today, which means, to basically *every* empire of today, which has to be stressed, because many of them live in the delusion that they aren't violent, that only the other empires are.
