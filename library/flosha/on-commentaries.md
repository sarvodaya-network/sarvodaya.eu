# On Commentaries

Vinoba clarified something for me, that I had misunderstood for a long time. He wrote:

> In Sanskrit literature, Gita, Vedanta-Sutra, Upanishads and Yoga-Sutra are the treatises on which numerous commentaries have been written, in this order. We find commentaries on Yoga-Sutra in different Indian languages, as well as in foreign languages. Among the five most important commentaries on the Yoga-Sutra, Vyasa’s commentary is considered the most authoritative. And again there are commentaries on the commentary of Vyasa as well.  
> Aphorisms are very short, consisting of two, three or four words. In those times, they did not have paper, so the guru would delineate a subject through an elaborate discourse, and after having explained the subject at length, he would put it in the condensed form of sutras, as an aid to memorize the whole subject. The disciple would learn the sutra (aphorism) by heart. As an example, there is a
sutra, “To keep the mind confined in one place is dharana.” The master would discuss this in a
long discourse. Hence in ancient times the concept of sect was sacred. In modern times, the connotation of “sect” has degenerated, but in those times it was a very sacred concept. The purpose of the sect was to preserve the knowledge that was acquired through the tradition of the guru-disciple lineage. Only if you had heard the discourse from the one who composed the aphorism were you considered the authority. Shankaracharya even says that one should disregard even a wise person if he does not belong to a sect. The meaning of the sutra or aphorism has a tradition. Without knowing the tradition, one cannot correctly interpret the aphorism or the terminology. One must know the traditional meanings of different words. If someone wants to give new meanings of one’s own liking to those sutras, that person should better write his own independent book. Hence, ancient commentators on the ancient sutras are regarded as more authoritative, because they had heard those sutras and their delineation from the
original composers themselves.

That said, before becoming aware of this I was under the impression, that these books with their extremely short and condensed aphorisms were meant to be considered like the major thing that one should study and try to understand, while I gave secondary importance to commentaries. But actually, the major thing was the explanation of the master that the aphorisms are only summarising for memory. Whoever has heard the explanations of the master, for him these short aphorisms suffice  and he can understand them; but for one who has not received any explanation, one cannot expect to understand the condensed summary. 

So it is also not that for some reason these men have written all cryptic in order to make it hard to understand or that we are stupid because we can't; it is simply the teaching condensed to the utmost for memory of those who have received the teaching, for them there is nothing cryptic about it; just if you haven't received the teaching, you won't understand.  
And since we may not have a teacher today who has received the teaching himself and can teach us, the commentaries of those who have shouldn't be considered to be secondary literature. They are actually primary literature and more important to read than the short sutras themselves, because only after having received the teaching in this way, in form of the commentaries, may the sutras themselves become valuable and understandable to you as a means to remind you of the teaching they contain.
