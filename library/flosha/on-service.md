# On Service 

Work + Love = Service.   
Service is the work of love. It is the only kind of work that is in accordance with love. Every work that is done out of love, in love, through love, without any expectation for reward, can be rightously called service. And whatever is not done out of love, in love, through love and not without any expectation for reward, that can not be called service in the sense in which we are using it here. It has to be pointed out, that when we use the word love, then we mean non-violent love. This kind of love, love without violence, love without desire, may as well be described as Benevolence (in German as *Barmherzigkeit*, in Sanskrit as *Maitrī*, or in Pali as *Mettā*), but when true love is meant to be like this and free from desire, then we can just call it love. And in the same way, when true work is meant to be service, because no work should be done without love, then we could as well just speak of *Work*. Any kind of work that isn't service, is "untrue", it is dishonest work. 

Now, who in this world is a burden on others? Is a poor begging man a burden on others? Is a wealthy, well-earned man a burden on ohters? The answer is very simple: Everyone, who uses anything that has to be made by others, is a burden on others. And the biggest burden on society is placed by those, who use up most. The one with the highest needs and the highest expenditure is the greatest burden on society and is the most dependent on others. Poor beggars on the streets do not use up much. Their needs are simple and little and their expenditure is very low. They are a burden on society like everyone else who uses things made by others, but their burden is so light that it can hardly be seen as a problem. Their dependence on others is very minor and neglectible. Those on the other hand that are considered to be "rich", those among them who use up very much, whose needs are many and whose expenditure is very high, they are obviously a much bigger burden on society, and they, while considering themselves as "independent" and "powerful", actually depend the most on others, very clearly so.  

There is a simple idea in ethics, by which one is supposed to evaluate whether an action is good or bad. It is by asking the question: "Would this still be good, if everyone would act like this?" This is a very valuable tool. So let us ask this question: "Would it be good, if everyone would beg?" Immediately someone jumps at the conclusion: "Of course not, when no one works and everyone begs for his living, then there is soon nothing to beg for anymore!" What a strange conclusion! Who has said anything about not working? Who has said anything about not producing? The question was: "Would it be good, if everyone would beg?" Or would that cause any kind of problem? The question is not: "Would it be good if everyone stops working and producing and begs *instead*?" But people automatically associate begging with "not-working". Which is a very strange and wrong assumption.  
Whether or not I work, whether or not I produce, whether or not I serve, has nothing to do with whether or not I earn money or beg instead. Begging is not the slightest in opposition to working; from begging doesn't follow that one doesn't work and from work doesn't follow that one doesn't beg. In the same way: From working doesn't follow that one earns money and from earning money doesn't follow that one works. This is so obvious that it shouldn't be necessary to point out, but it is very necessary, as it is in exactly in the ignorance of such evident insights that most confusion and conflict is born.  

(1) I can work very hard, many hours a day, e.g. in a prison colony, and earn no money whatsoever nor beg for my living. I am just forced to do that and kept alive by the prison guards to do it. That is one possible way of doing it. (2) I can work very hard, many hours a day, e.g. in a company, and "earn" some money for my living. I may do that deliberately. I may as well be forced to do it; just because I signed a contract doesn't mean that I am working there deliberately, if the conditions of my life and the availability or inavailability of work, due to my dependence on money, are forcing me to do it. That is another possible way of doing it. (3) I can as well *not* work at all or not do anything that can be considered to be worthy of reward, and yet get much money for whatever reason. I may just inherit it. I may find it somewhere. I may steal it. I may be given it by someone else. There are many possible scenarios in which I can have a lot of money while doing nothing in return. That is another possible way of doing it. (4) I can also *not* work at all and not serve in any way, and just beg for my living. This can be done out of laziness. This can be done out of helplessness. It may be done due to an unwillingness to work. It may as well be done due to an inability to work. This is another possible way of doing it. Lastly (5) I can also *work* very much, serve others very much, earn no money whatsoever and *yet* not being forced to do that, but doing it voluntarily and then decide to beg. This is another possible way of doing it.

This last way, of all the possibilities, is the one that people nowadays consider the most strange, as almost unimagineable. It is not only a possible way. It is, very clearly so, the most pure, the most innocent, the most selfless way of them all. And is doing it like this unrealistic? Why would it be? In fact, it is done all the time. Everyone who is doing any kind of voluntary work, any kind of real service with no reward (e.g. caring for others voluntarily, giving food to the poor, working in local clubs and initiatives, such as in many sports), all of them do it in this kind of way. They work hard for no material reward. Medieval begging monks were beggars, they had no money, earned no money, and yet they did manual labour and service. They cared for the poor, they worked hard to construct chapels or churches, they worked on the fields, but they didn't want a reward for it. They wanted to survive and get what they need by opening up their hands asking for the benevolence of others, in order to stand up the next day to continue their service. What could be wrong about this way of life? Isn't this the most right livelihood imagineable? It has also been done for the majority of "civilization" by women. They served all day. They helped all day. But they usually didn't provide for the food by earning money. Didn't they have a right to open their hands and beg to be given their fair share? 

Now in modern times they want to put women into this same position of earning "their own money" and they consider this as liberation. Instead of bringing women into this position of men, they should have brought men out of it too.
