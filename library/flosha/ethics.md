Aguéli:
> “This is how I understand a modern monotheism: fanatical towards oneself, tolerant towards others... It is a balance within the self and not outside of it. Whoever has his centre of gravity exclusively in exterior things is a fetishist.”

> This radical interiorisation of the process of emancipation aligns with Ibn ʿArabi’s treatment of practical virtues. In the second section of his Futuhat, dealing with ethics (faṣl al-muʿāmalāt), each chapter dedicated to a particular virtue is followed by another discussing its renunciation (tark), which entails the internal reintegration of the objectivities of ethical consciousness. (Akbarian Anarchism: Ivan Aguéli (d. 1917) on Islam, Freedom and Shariʿa by Gregory Vandamme)

> Aguéli appears to draw inspiration from this logic when discussing the notion of humility: “It means nothing to be humble or not, as we are all nothingness. They have turned humility into a virtue, a goal, whereas it is merely a means, an exercise, and a form of training. It is just a small station on the journey, where one stops as needed. Vanity is foolishness. Misplaced humility can be equally so.”

> Aguéli draws on an expression dear to Ibn ʿArabi, deﬁning wisdom (ḥikma) as “the art of placing each thing in its rightful place.” This ontological hierarchy ﬁnds its counterpart in Aguéli’s vision of a social hierarchy, one not based on privileges but rather on the responsibilities that wisdom demands in proportion to its realisation: “The Arab social principle is both fraternal and aristocratic. The wealthy, the learned, and the strong bear duties toward the poor, the ignorant, and the weak.” The ideal society described by Aguéli is thus far from being anarchist. On the contrary, he characterises it as an “Islamic aristo-democracy.” 

What the author describes here as "being far from anarchist" can be perfectly anarchist, when considering what these duties are that the aristocrats bear.

The duty of the wealthy is to help others to overcome poverty.
The duty of the strong is to help others to overcome weakness.
The duty of the learned is to help others to overcome ignorance.

If thereby the "aritocrats" fulfill their duty, everyone will become an aristocrat and by everyone being an aristocrat, the aristo-democracy is in fact anarchist.

> Aguéli’s conception of shariʿa encapsulates the entirety of his spiritual and intellectual approach. He refers to what he sees as both the foundation and the ultimate aim of the shariʿa as “lordly freedom” (liberté dominicale), in the sense of sovereign or divine freedom. This freedom is “original, innate, extra-temporal” and always exists in the individual: “It cannot be destroyed, it is inevitable, as it constitutes the reason for each person’s existence.” 

> According to Aguéli, the shariʿa seeks to preserve and cultivate this freedom, not to restrict it: “The Law that acknowledges this secret, as well as its inaccessible, inviolable, and incommunicable nature, guarantees the most precious of humanity’s four cardinal freedoms, for it is the expression of the highest form of life.”

> Aguéli: “Mohyiddin ibn Arabi refers to exclusivists — that is, fanatics and those astray — as those who exhort you to be like them and do as they do in all things, failing to respect the legitimate freedom of the individual. Everything comes from God: the disbelief of the faithful as well as the faith of the believer. Any zeal outside of public matters is an inconsiderate act, committed by those with a crude understanding of God’s power.”
