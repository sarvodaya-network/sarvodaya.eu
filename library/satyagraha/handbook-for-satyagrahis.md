## INTRODUCTION 

Ome of Gandhi's grest contributions to the world wss his method of working. He introduced nonviolence as an effective tool of social change. To a world used to the incentives of greed and fear, Gandhi gave an alternative: the driving force of love. Love had been used earlier too for bringing about change in individual human behaviour, but Gandhi used this force for social change. He used it both at the individual level and at the level of wider society. He used it with great success particularly in the political field, in the fight against the injustice of colonialism and imperialism.  
Gandhi called his new tool SATYAGRAHA which literally meant insistence on or adherence to truth. To Gandhi his own long and colourful life was an experiment in truth. Love of nonviolence came to him as a corollary of his search for truth. This method of Satyagraha was used by him for over half a century in two continents for the liberation of some of the most oppressed people.  
Satyagraha may remain for future generations Gandhi's great legacy to mankind. In one way or the other it has been tried with greater or lesser effectiveness by millions of people in a number of countries in a score of different situations, such as apartheid, struggle for civil liberties, untouchability, oppression of the rich over the poor, and outbursts of violence among communities. In fact, this new tool of social change has attracted a countless number of people in fields as wide and varied as father-son relationship to foreign aggression.  
To give the reader a clear idea about Gandhi's concept of Satyagraha, or nonviolent direct action, we quote below some passages from Gandhi's own writings spread over a number of years:  

> Satyagraha is a process of educating public opinion, such that it covers all the elements of society and in the end makes itself irresistible. Violence interrupts the process and prolongs the real revolution of the whole social structure.¹

> With Satya combined with Ahimsa, you can bring the world to your feet. Satyagraha in its essence is nothing but the introduction of truth and gentleness in the political, i.e. the national life.²  

> In the application of Satyagaha, I discovered in the earliest stages that pursuit of Truth did not admit of violence being inflicted on one's opponent, but that he must be weaned from error by patience and sympathy. For what appears to be truth to one may appear to be error to the other. And patience means self-suffering. So the doctrine came to mean vindication of Truth, not by infliction of suffering on the opponent but one's own self.³

> The hardest heart and the grossest ignorance must disappear before the rising sun of suffering without anger and without malice.⁴

Some of the most notable examples in post-independence India of the practice of Gandhi's method of change through love have been: the Bhoodan-Gramdan (land-gift and "villagisation" of land), and Shanti Sena movements initiated by Vinoba Bhave; and the movement for Total Revolution enunciated by Jayaprakash Narayan. What Gandhi did in the political field Vinoba, in his own gentle way, tried to do in the economic field. His movement for Bhoodan and Gramdan was a novel method of introducing nonviolence in the economic life of India, i.e., in the relationship between the landowner and the landless labourer. His method was that of persuasion and persuasion alone. Jayaprakash Narayan introduced the element of resistance to evil in his movement for Total Revolution. Thousands of young men and women were willing to undergo all kinds of hardship under Jayaprakash Narayan's leadership to fight corruption, injustice and oppression. Satyagraha was the tool that was used by Jayaprakash Narayan in his Bihar movement although in his humility he often refrained from using this term for his method of operation: It is through this method of nonviolent direct action that Jayaprakash Narayan is indisputably linked with Gandhi. That is the reason why in the title of this book I have linked Gandhi's term 'Satyagraha' with Jayaprakash Narayan's term 'Total Revolution' (*Purna Kranti*).  
There have been efforts to organize these volunteers as Bhoodan-Gramdan workers, *Lok Sevaks*, *Shanti Sainiks*, *Tarun Shanti Sainiks* or volunteers of *Chhatra Yuva Sangharsha Vahini*. Efforts have also been made to evolve methods of training for these volunteers. The author, who has been actively engaged in the organization and training of these volunteers of peace, has prepared three Handbooks for the guidance of *Shanti Sainiks*, Shanti Sena Instructors, and Organizers of the JP Movement in Bihar. The present *Handbook* has been prepared more or less in the style of its three predecessors, but it is meant for a wider range of people interested in nonviolence. It is meant particularly for the volunteers at present engaged in the peaceful movement for Total Revolution, as also generally for anyone interested in the effective day-to-day use of non-violence.  
I have drawn fully from my previous three Handbooks and also from other sources, particularly from Gandhi. Whenever there is a direct quotation at some length, I have acknowledged it in a footnore. Suggestions and criticisms of interested readers are most welcome.
